1. Introduction

What a model architecture is

A model architecture is the way a model is wired: how information enters, how it moves between the model's parts, and how the answer comes out. Two models can share an architecture and still behave very differently, because what they learn depends on the data they were trained on. The architecture is the shape of the brain; the data is what it learned.

Why ASR, NMT and TTS together

These are the three model stages of a typical Indic voice AI system. A caller speaks in their own language, the system turns that into text, translates it if needed, works out a reply, and speaks it back. Each stage handles a different kind of data, which is why each settled on a different design.

2. The building blocks

What a neural network is

A neural network is a large set of simple calculators arranged in layers. Each one takes in numbers, multiplies them by its own learned values (its parameters), and passes the result on. Training means showing the network many examples and nudging those values until its answers come out right. Everything a network "knows" is stored in those values.

Because networks only work with numbers, every word or sound slice is first turned into a list of numbers called an embedding. Words with similar meanings end up with similar lists.

What attention means

Attention lets each word look at every other word in the input and decide how much each one matters to it, however far apart they are. This is how a model links a verb to its subject at the other end of a long sentence.

Encoder vs decoder

Most of the models in this guide are built from one or both of these two parts.

Does every model have both?

No. Each stage of the pipeline tends to follow its own pattern.

In short, the stages that write text lean on decoders (LLM, NMT), the stage that has to understand audio leans on the encoder (ASR), and TTS sits in between because it both reads text and produces sound.

A naming trap. The words also have a second meaning. In compression models such as audio codecs, the "encoder" squeezes sound into codes and the "decoder" rebuilds the sound from them. So the "Vocos decoder" in Indic-Speak rebuilds audio; it is not a decoder that writes words.

Tokens and audio codecs

Models do not read whole sentences. Text is cut into tokens, usually word pieces. Audio can also be turned into tokens by an audio codec, a model that compresses sound into a short list of codes and can rebuild the sound from them. Codecs are what let a text-style model generate speech.

Why audio and text need a different design than images

An image

Fixed size. Every part is available at once. Order does not change meaning: a cat is a cat wherever it sits in the picture.

Audio and text

Variable length. Order changes meaning ("dog bites man" vs "man bites dog"). Meaning depends on what came before and after. Audio is also very long: about 100 slices a second for ASR and 24,000 samples a second for output speech.

So sequence models need two things images do not: a sense of order, and a way to connect pieces that are far apart. Attention provides both, which is why the transformer took over.

3. Is the transformer really "best" for Indic languages?

Short answer: it is an empirical pattern, not a mathematical proof. Across most benchmarks for Indian languages, transformer-based models currently score best. That is a statement about results so far, not a law.

Why it tends to win

Indian languages have relatively free word order and rich morphology, meaning a lot of grammar is packed into word endings (tense, gender, case, politeness). Getting a sentence right often depends on linking words that sit far apart. Attention connects any two words directly, while older step-by-step designs like RNNs had to carry that link through every word in between and frequently lost it. Transformers also train in parallel, which made training on very large datasets practical.

Why it wins harder for Indic specifically

Transformers made large-scale multilingual pretraining practical. One model can learn from many languages at once, and a low-resource language like Bhili can borrow patterns from a better-resourced relative like Marathi: shared vocabulary, similar grammar, and the same Devanagari script. For languages with very little data of their own, this borrowing matters more than any other single factor.

A factor that is easy to miss: the tokenizer

How a model cuts text into tokens matters as much as its architecture. Tokenizers built mostly on English split Indic words into many tiny pieces, so the model sees more fragments, runs slower, and understands less. Models with tokenizers trained on Indian languages (IndicTrans2, Sarvam's models) do noticeably better for the same size.

The honest caveats

  • "Best family" is not "any model". A transformer trained on little or poorly matched data will still perform badly. The pretraining and fine-tuning data decide the result for a given language.
  • Plain transformers are rarely used alone for speech. ASR models add convolution layers to catch fine sound detail, and TTS models add vocoders and codecs to produce audio. Section 8 explains why.
  • It may not stay this way. State space models such as Mamba are competitive for long, streaming audio, where transformers become expensive.

4. ASR: speech to text

What came beforeUntil about 2012, speech recognition used statistical models with hand-built pronunciation dictionaries. Neural networks first replaced parts of that pipeline, then replaced the whole thing with single models that go straight from audio to text.

Three questions every ASR model answers

5. NMT: translation

What came beforeStatistical systems translated in phrase-sized chunks and produced choppy output. RNN encoder-decoders (2014) made translation fluent, attention (2015) fixed long sentences, and the transformer (2017) made everything faster and better.

Note that the first two designs below share the same architecture. The difference between them is the data they were trained on, which is exactly the point made in section 3.

The original 2017 transformer, trained for a single language pair on that pair's sentence pairs only. It works well when millions of sentence pairs exist, which is rarely true for Indian languages, and very rarely for tribal languages.

Indic examples. IndicTrans2 (AI4Bharat, all 22 scheduled languages); NLLB-200 (Meta).

Languages outside its training set, such as Bhili, are not covered without further training.

How it works. A general LLM, already pretrained on huge amounts of multilingual text, is fine-tuned on translation pairs. Translation becomes "continue this text in the other language".

Indic examples. Sarvam-Translate (built on Gemma 3); Indic-Translate (Bodhan AI and AI4Bharat).

Wins
Uses wider context, follows style instructions, and adapts to a new language from a small dataset.
Struggles
Larger and costlier to run. Can add or drop content, so outputs need checking.

6. TTS: text to speech

What came beforeOlder systems stitched together recorded sound units, or generated speech from statistics. Both sounded robotic and needed many studio hours per voice. WaveNet (2016) produced the first natural machine speech, but was far too slow for real use.

Most TTS systems have two jobs: turn text into a "sound picture" (a mel spectrogram) or sound tokens, then turn that into an actual audio wave using a vocoder.

7. The steps between the models

ASR, NMT and TTS do the heavy lifting, but a real voice agent also runs a set of smaller helper steps between them. These steps decide whether the system works on real phone calls, with background noise, mixed languages, numbers and pauses. When a voice agent misbehaves, one of these helpers is often the cause rather than the main model.

10. The common thread

All three domains now share the same foundation, the transformer and its attention mechanism, and each adds what its kind of data needs.

Transformerattention, large-scale multilingual pretraining
ASRAdds what audio input needs:
  • Convolution for fine sound detail (Conformer)
  • CTC or RNN-T for live output
  • Self-supervised pretraining for scarce transcripts
NMTMostly the transformer as is:
  • Encoder-decoder for scheduled languages
  • Decoder-only LLMs for low-resource languages
  • Gains come mainly from data
TTSAdds what audio output needs:
  • Duration and pitch prediction
  • Audio codecs and sound tokens
  • A vocoder to make the wave

The pattern behind every change in this guide is the same: an approach hit a limit (speed, data, or naturalness) and the next design was built to get past it. For Indian languages the limit that matters most today is data, which is why multilingual pretraining and careful fine-tuning matter more than choosing between architectures.

11. Glossary

Model terms

Architecture
The overall design and internal structure of a neural network.
Parameters
The adjustable internal values a model learns during training, roughly a model's "knowledge". More parameters usually means more capacity to learn, but also more computing power needed. "3B" means 3 billion parameters.
Encoder
The part of a model that reads and understands an input before anything is generated.
Decoder
The part of a model that generates output, usually one piece at a time.
Attention
A mechanism that lets a model weigh how much every part of its input matters to every other part, regardless of distance.
Embedding
A way of representing a word, sound or token as a list of numbers that captures its meaning, so the model can do math on language.
Token
The smallest unit a model processes. For text, a whole word, part of a word, or a character, depending on the model. For audio, a short code standing for a slice of sound.
Tokenization
Breaking raw text (or audio) into tokens before feeding it into a model. Tokenizers built mainly for English split Indian-language words into many small pieces, which makes models slower and less accurate.
Context window
The maximum amount of text or audio a model can consider at once. For many ASR models this is about 30 seconds of audio.
Mixture of experts (MoE)
A design where only a small subset of a model's parameters is used for any given input, making large models cheaper to run.
Convolutional neural network (CNN)
A network that is especially good at catching small, local patterns. Originally built for recognising shapes in images, also used for short bursts of sound.
Recurrent neural network (RNN)
An older design that processes a sequence step by step, carrying a "memory" from one step to the next. LSTM and GRU are improved versions that remember longer.
Conformer
A speech encoder that combines convolution (for fine sound detail) with attention (for wider context) in every layer.
Mel spectrogram
A picture of sound showing which pitches are loud at each moment. Most speech models use it as their input or as a middle step.
Audio codec
A model that compresses audio into a short list of tokens and can rebuild the audio from them. Lets text-style models generate speech.
Vocoder
The final step of TTS that turns a sound picture or sound tokens into an actual audio wave.
GAN (generative adversarial network)
A training setup with a "maker" network and a "judge" network. The judge tries to spot fakes, which pushes the maker to produce realistic output. Most fast vocoders are trained this way.
VAE (variational autoencoder)
A model that compresses data into a flexible space where nearby points sound or look similar, so it can produce natural variation.
State space model
A newer sequence design, such as Mamba, that reads input step by step with a fixed-size memory, keeping delay low on long inputs.

Training terms

Pretraining
The initial, broad phase of training on large amounts of general data, before the model is specialised for a task.
Fine-tuning
Training an already pretrained model further on a smaller, task-specific dataset, such as Marathi to Bhili sentence pairs.
Transfer learning
The idea that knowledge learned on one task or language helps a model on a different but related task or language.
Supervised learning
Training with labelled examples, where the correct answer is provided alongside each input.
Self-supervised learning
Training a model to predict hidden parts of its own input (a hidden word or audio chunk), without manually labelled data.
Parallel data
Pairs of the same sentence in two languages, used to train translation models.
Low-resource language
A language with little digital text or audio available for training, such as Bhili.
Code-mixing
Switching between languages within one sentence, such as Hindi and English.
Loss function
A mathematical measure of how wrong a model's prediction is, used to guide training.
Overfitting
When a model learns the training data too specifically and performs poorly on new, unseen data. A common risk when fine-tuning on small datasets.
Checkpoint
A saved snapshot of a model's parameters at a point during training, which can be reloaded later.
Distillation
Training a smaller, faster model to copy a larger one. IndicTrans2's "distilled" versions are made this way.
Quantization
Storing a model's parameters with fewer digits to make it smaller and faster, with a small loss in quality. Common for running models on phones.

Inference and testing terms

Inference
Using an already-trained model to produce an output on new input, as opposed to training it. Training happens once, or occasionally when a model is updated; inference happens every single time someone uses the model, for example every time a sentence is translated or a voice command is transcribed. Inference is far cheaper and faster than training because the model's values are fixed and it is only running a calculation, not learning. It can run on a large server (common for bigger models) or directly on a phone or small device (common for lighter, optimised models). The speed of inference decides whether a voice assistant feels instant or sluggish.
Endpoint
The address a piece of software sends a request to in order to use a deployed model and get a response back, the "doorway" through which an application talks to a model. For example, a translation app sends the typed sentence to a translation model's endpoint and receives the translation back. Most production AI features work this way; the user never sees the model, only the app that talks to it through the endpoint.
Latency
The delay between giving a model an input and receiving its output. Critical for real-time voice applications.
Time to first audio
For TTS, how long before the first sound plays. Matters more than total generation time in conversation.
Streaming
Processing input, such as audio, continuously as it arrives rather than waiting for all of it before starting.
Autoregressive
Generating output one piece at a time, where each new piece depends on everything generated before it.
Non-autoregressive
Generating the entire output at once, in parallel, rather than step by step.
Connectionist temporal classification (CTC)
An output method for speech recognition that labels each slice of audio with a character or a "blank", then merges repeats and drops blanks. It lines up audio with text even though they have different lengths. Fast and simple, but each guess is made without looking at the others, so grammar is weaker.
RNN transducer (RNN-T)
An output method for speech recognition designed for real-time streaming. A small prediction network keeps track of the words written so far and combines with the audio encoder at each step, so output appears word by word as the person speaks. Despite the name, it is now usually paired with Conformer encoders rather than RNNs.
Voice activity detection (VAD)
A small model that detects whether someone is speaking, so silence and background noise are not sent to ASR.
Turn-taking (endpointing)
Deciding when a caller has finished their turn and the agent should reply. Simple systems wait for a fixed length of silence; newer ones use small models that also consider tone and words.
Text normalisation
Converting written forms such as numbers, currency and dates into the words a TTS model should say, for example ₹2,500 into "do hazaar paanch sau rupaye".
G2P (grapheme to phoneme)
Converting spelling (graphemes) into speech sounds (phonemes), so a TTS model knows how to pronounce a word.
Schwa deletion
In Hindi, Marathi and some other languages, the short "a" sound written into many letters is often not spoken, as in "कमल" said "kamal". TTS models must learn or be told when to drop it.
Diarization
Working out "who spoke when" in a recording with several speakers.
Language identification
Detecting which language is being spoken.
Inverse text normalisation
Converting spoken forms into written ones, such as "twenty five" into "25".
Hallucination
When a model outputs content that was not in the input, such as words that were never spoken or facts added to a translation.
Word error rate (WER)
The main ASR accuracy measure: substituted, missing and extra words, divided by the number of words in the correct transcript. Lower is better, and it can exceed 100% if the model adds many extra words.
Character error rate (CER)
The same idea as WER, counted in characters. Often fairer for Indian languages.
BLEU (bilingual evaluation understudy)
A translation quality measure comparing a model's output to a reference translation by word overlap. Higher usually means closer to correct. "dBLEU" is a document-level variant used in some recent evaluations.
chrF and chrF++
Translation measures based on character overlap rather than whole words. Better suited to Indian languages with rich word endings.
MOS (mean opinion score)
A TTS quality measure where listeners rate speech from 1 to 5.
Real-time factor (RTF)
Processing time divided by audio length. Below 1 means faster than real time.
Benchmark
A standard test or dataset used to measure and compare model performance.
Zero-shot
When a model does a task it was never specifically trained on, relying on what it learned elsewhere. In TTS, "zero-shot voice cloning" means copying a voice from a short sample without training on that voice.
